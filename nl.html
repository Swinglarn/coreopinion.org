Politics in the Netherlands: the landscape behind this test

The Netherlands uses one of the purest proportional systems in the world. The entire country is a single constituency and there is no electoral threshold beyond the roughly 0.67 per cent needed for one of the 150 seats. Fifteen or more parties routinely win representation.

A fragmented party system

The main families include the liberal-conservative VVD, the social democratic and green left grouping, the centrist D66, the Christian democratic CDA, and the right-wing populist PVV. Alongside them sit parties for animal welfare, farmers, pensioners, and specific religious traditions, several of which have held seats for decades.

No party comes close to a majority, so coalitions of four or more are normal and formation talks routinely run for months. The 2021 formation took a record 299 days. Coalition agreements are consequently detailed and constraining.

The cleavages that actually divide voters

Immigration and integration have been central since the early 2000s and have restructured the party system more than any other issue. Nitrogen emissions policy, driven by EU environmental rules and bearing heavily on an intensive agricultural sector, triggered sustained farmer protests and produced a farmers' party that entered parliament with significant support.

Housing shortages in the Randstad, the densely populated western conurbation, and the future of the pension system are the other persistent domestic arguments. EU policy is contested in detail rather than in principle, given how central the single market is to a trade-dependent economy.

How this maps onto the compass

The Netherlands combines an extensive welfare state and universal insurance-based healthcare with a strongly market-oriented, trade-dependent economy and a large private pension system. It sits near the economic centre by European standards. Socially it has been among the most permissive countries in the world for decades, having legalised same-sex marriage first and maintaining liberal drug and end-of-life policies, so the governance axis is where Dutch politics divides most sharply.
