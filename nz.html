Politics in New Zealand: the landscape behind this test

New Zealand replaced first-past-the-post with mixed-member proportional representation in 1996, and the change reshaped its politics. Voters cast two votes, one for a local candidate and one for a party, with the party vote determining overall seat share. Single-party majorities are now rare, so governing means negotiating.

The party system

Labour and National remain the two largest parties and the only realistic sources of a prime minister, but neither can usually govern alone. The Greens sit to Labour's left on economics and environment. ACT advances a classical liberal, small-state programme. New Zealand First occupies a populist position that has supported governments of both major parties. Te Pati Maori advocates for Maori rights and constitutional change.

Because coalition partners are needed, smaller parties translate modest vote shares into real policy concessions, and coalition agreements are published documents that shape the government's programme.

The cleavages that actually divide voters

The Treaty of Waitangi, signed in 1840, is central to New Zealand's constitutional arrangements and to its most contested arguments. Questions about co-governance, the role of te reo Maori in public life, and how Treaty obligations bind government are genuinely divisive in a way that has no close parallel abroad.

Housing affordability is persistently among the most severe in the developed world and dominates domestic debate. Agriculture's central economic role also makes climate policy unusually complicated, since a large share of emissions comes from farming rather than energy.

How this maps onto the compass

New Zealand built an extensive welfare state, then executed one of the most rapid market liberalisations in the OECD during the 1980s. The result is a mixed settlement: public healthcare and income support alongside comparatively open markets. On social questions the country is permissive by international standards.
