National Socialism

Overview

National Socialism, commonly referred to as Nazism, is a totalitarian political ideology that emerged in Germany during the interwar period. It is a radical variant of fascism that combines extreme nationalism, social Darwinism, militarism, and systemic antisemitism. The ideology rejects both liberal representative democracy and parliamentary capitalism, as well as Marxist socialism, proposing instead a racially unified national community led by a single charismatic dictator.

The defining characteristic of National Socialism is its pseudo-scientific racial hierarchy, which asserts that the Germanic peoples constitute an Aryan master race entitled to dominate others. Individual liberties are entirely rejected, with all citizens expected to subordinate their personal desires, economic activities, and social lives to the interests of the state and the preservation of the racial collective. This absolute subordination is encapsulated in the slogan, "The common good before the individual good."

On the political compass, National Socialism occupies a distinct position in the upper-middle region. While it is strongly authoritarian, its economic policies are mixed rather than purely free-market or collectivist. The regime permitted private property and capitalist enterprise, but subjected them to strict state planning, price controls, and production targets, positioning it as economically centrist with extreme authoritarian governance.

Historical Development

National Socialism developed in the aftermath of World War I, fueled by German resentment over the Treaty of Versailles (1919), economic instability, and fear of communism. The National Socialist German Workers' Party (NSDAP) was founded in Munich in 1920, and Adolf Hitler assumed leadership shortly thereafter, drafting the party's 25-point program which combined nationalist and anti-capitalist demands.

Following the failed Munich Putsch of 1923, Hitler used his imprisonment to write Mein Kampf, outlining his racial theories and expansionist foreign policy. The Great Depression of 1929 provided the economic crisis that propelled the party from a fringe movement to a major electoral force. By promising economic recovery, national pride, and order, the NSDAP won the support of middle-class voters, industrialists, and military officers who feared a communist revolution.

Hitler was appointed Chancellor in January 1933. Within months, the Reichstag Fire Decree and the Enabling Act dismantled the Weimar Republic, banned opposition parties, and established a one-party dictatorship. The regime quickly implemented the policy of Gleichschaltung, bringing all civic organizations, churches, and schools under Nazi control. Rearmament programs funded through massive deficit spending rapidly reduced unemployment, reinforcing popular support for the regime.

Antisemitism was institutionalized through the Nuremberg Laws of 1935, which stripped Jewish citizens of civil rights, leading to the state-sponsored violence of Kristallnacht in 1938. The regime's foreign policy aimed to secure Lebensraum (living space) in Eastern Europe, leading to the annexation of Austria and Czechoslovakia, and finally the invasion of Poland in September 1939, initiating World War II. During the war, the regime executed the Holocaust, a systematic, industrial genocide resulting in the deaths of six million Jews and millions of Soviet prisoners, Romani, disabled persons, and political dissidents. The regime collapsed with Germany's unconditional surrender in May 1945, after which the Nuremberg trials exposed the scale of its crimes and banned the ideology.

Core Beliefs and Principles

The core of National Socialist ideology is racial determinism. Rosenberg and other party theorists argued that human history is not a struggle between economic classes, but a struggle for survival between biological races. Germanic peoples were placed at the apex of this hierarchy, while Jews, Romani, and Slavic peoples were categorized as subhuman. This biological determinism justified expansionism and genocide as natural expressions of social Darwinism, where the strong inevitably conquer the weak.

The Führerprinzip, or leader principle, is the foundational organizational concept of the ideology. It establishes that authority flows downward from a single, absolute leader whose decisions are not subject to democratic checks, constitutional limits, or judicial review. Citizens and party members owed absolute obedience to the Führer, whose will was considered the ultimate source of law.

Volksgemeinschaft, or the people's community, was the ideal social order. It aimed to unite Germans across traditional class divisions into a unified racial collective. This unity, however, was strictly exclusionary, defined by the exclusion and persecution of anyone deemed racially, politically, or physically unfit. The family was idealized as the basic unit of the nation, with state policies actively encouraging high birth rates among those deemed racially pure.

Lebensraum, or living space, was the geographical goal of the regime. Hitler argued that Germany's population growth required the conquest of agricultural and raw-material-producing territories in Eastern Europe. The native populations of these territories were to be displaced, starved, or enslaved to make way for German settlers, creating a self-sufficient continental empire protected from global economic blockade.

"The state must subordinate all individual, social, and economic activity to the preservation and expansion of the racial nation."

Economic Policy

National Socialist economic policy was highly pragmatic and subordinated to political and military goals. The regime rejected both Marxist nationalization of all industries and laissez-faire capitalist deregulation, pursuing instead a third path that maintained private property but controlled its operation through intensive state planning and cartels.

The state actively promoted the privatization of state-owned railways, banks, and steel mills in the 1930s, a policy that consolidated economic power in the hands of a few large industrial conglomerates. In exchange for monopolies and guaranteed profits, these corporations cooperated with the state's Four-Year Plan, directed by Hermann Göring, which prioritized rearmament, synthetic raw material production, and autarky.

Labor relations were completely restructured. Independent trade unions were banned in May 1933 and replaced by the German Labor Front (DAF), a state-run organization that managed wages, working hours, and workplace discipline. The Strength through Joy program provided subsidized vacations and cultural activities to workers to maintain morale and productivity. The regime also utilized massive forced labor programs, exploiting millions of civilian workers and concentration camp prisoners in armaments factories as the war progressed.

Price controls, wage freezes, and import restrictions were introduced to prevent inflation and manage foreign exchange reserves. While private industrialists retained ownership of their firms, they lost the freedom to set prices, choose raw materials, or hire workers without state approval. Capital investment was directed toward war-related industries, and consumer goods production was severely restricted, establishing a command economy that maintained the nominal structure of private capitalism.

Economic Position Summary

State-directed capitalism where private property is maintained but completely subordinated to national rearmament and state production targets.

Governance and Freedom

Governance under National Socialism was totalitarian, characterized by the eradication of the boundary between public administration and private life. The state maintained control through a combination of popular mobilization, bureaucratic centralization, and systemic terror. The constitution of the Weimar Republic was never formally abolished, but it was rendered meaningless by emergency decrees that suspended all basic civil rights.

The state apparatus operated alongside the Nazi Party organization, creating a system of dual power that fostered administrative competition and inefficiency. Hitler encouraged this overlap, allowing rival agencies to compete for his approval, which ensured that his personal decision remained the final arbiter in all policy disputes. The civil service was purged of political opponents and non-Aryans in 1933, ensuring absolute administrative compliance.

State security was maintained by the SS, led by Heinrich Himmler, which integrated the police force and the Gestapo (secret police) into a single apparatus operating outside the jurisdiction of ordinary courts. The judicial system was subverted through the creation of Special Courts and the People's Court, which prosecuted political crimes and treason with minimal procedural protections, frequently issuing death sentences.

Public information and culture were tightly controlled by the Ministry of Public Propaganda. All journalists, artists, musicians, and writers were required to join state-sponsored chambers, which functioned as regulatory bodies to enforce censorship and promote ideology. Radio ownership was subsidized, and loudspeakers were installed in public squares to ensure that the regime's message reached the entire population, creating an environment of perpetual ideological mobilization.

Governance Position Summary

Totalitarian dictatorship, absolute suppression of democratic institutions and civil liberties, and systemic racial discrimination.

Notable Figures in National Socialism

Adolf Hitler

1889 - 1945

Führer of Nazi Germany

Hitler led the National Socialist German Workers' Party to power in 1933, establishing a totalitarian dictatorship. His aggressive expansionist foreign policy initiated World War II in Europe, and his regime executed the systematic genocide of six million Jews and millions of others in the Holocaust. He committed suicide in Berlin in April 1945 as Soviet forces entered the city.

Joseph Goebbels

1897 - 1945

Reich Minister of Public Enlightenment and Propaganda

Goebbels served as the chief propagandist of the Nazi regime, utilizing radio, press, and cinema to mobilize the German population and promote antisemitism. He advocated for total war in a famous 1943 speech and remained fiercely loyal to Hitler, committing suicide with his family in the Führerbunker shortly after Hitler's death.

Alfred Rosenberg

1893 - 1946

Chief racial theorist of the Nazi Party

Rosenberg authored The Myth of the Twentieth Century (1930), which provided the pseudo-scientific justification for Aryan racial supremacy, antisemitism, and Lebensraum. He later directed the systematic looting of Jewish art and cultural property throughout occupied Europe and was executed in 1946 following the Nuremberg trials.

Gregor Strasser

1892 - 1934

Co-leader of the socialist-leaning wing of the NSDAP

Strasser championed the anti-capitalist, socialist-leaning wing of the early Nazi Party, often clashing with Hitler over economic policy. Despite his organizational skills in building the party's infrastructure, he was marginalized as Hitler consolidated power and was assassinated during the Night of the Long Knives purge in 1934.
