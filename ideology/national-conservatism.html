National Conservatism

What is national conservatism? Explore the history and principles of national conservatism from Hamilton to Orban, its focus on sovereignty and tradition, and its place on the political compass.

Overview

National conservatism is a political ideology that fuses traditional conservative values with a strong emphasis on national sovereignty, cultural cohesion, and the primacy of the nation-state as the organizing unit of political life. It rejects both the cosmopolitan universalism of liberal internationalism and the purely economic focus of libertarian-leaning free-market conservatism. For national conservatives, the nation is not merely an administrative unit but a community bound together by shared history, language, religion, and customs, and the state exists to protect and perpetuate that community against external threats and internal dissolution.

National conservatism differs from nationalism in its explicitly conservative orientation. While nationalism can take left-wing or revolutionary forms, as in many anti-colonial liberation movements, national conservatism pairs its defense of the nation with a commitment to established social hierarchies, traditional moral values, and institutional continuity. It also differs from neoconservatism, which advocates the active promotion of democratic values abroad through military intervention and international institutions. National conservatives are generally skeptical of such projects, preferring a foreign policy that prioritizes the national interest over ideological crusades.

The ideology occupies a distinctive space on the political spectrum. It endorses market capitalism but rejects the laissez-faire orthodoxy that treats national borders as obstacles to economic efficiency. Strategic tariffs, restrictions on foreign ownership of critical infrastructure, and industrial policy to support domestic manufacturing are all consistent with the national conservative economic vision. On questions of governance, national conservatism favors strong executive authority, robust border enforcement, and policies that reinforce traditional family structures and religious institutions.

Historical Development

The intellectual roots of national conservatism extend to the earliest debates over the relationship between state power and national identity. Alexander Hamilton, as the first Secretary of the United States Treasury, articulated a vision of economic nationalism in his Report on Manufactures (1791) that called for protective tariffs and government support for domestic industry. Hamilton argued that the United States could not achieve true political independence without economic self-sufficiency, a position that placed him in direct conflict with Thomas Jefferson's agrarian vision and preference for free trade. Hamilton's program, which included the establishment of a national bank and the federal assumption of state debts, laid the foundation for the American System of economics later championed by Henry Clay and Abraham Lincoln.

In Europe, the 19th century saw the development of conservative nationalism in response to the upheavals of the French Revolution and the Napoleonic Wars. Benjamin Disraeli, who served as British Prime Minister in 1868 and again from 1874 to 1880, articulated a vision of "One Nation" conservatism that combined imperial patriotism with social reform aimed at binding the working class to the national community. In Germany, Otto von Bismarck unified the German states under Prussian leadership through a combination of diplomatic maneuvering and military force, then implemented a system of social insurance designed to undercut the appeal of socialism while reinforcing loyalty to the new nation-state.

The 20th century produced several prominent expressions of national conservatism. Charles de Gaulle built the institutions of the French Fifth Republic around a powerful presidency and pursued an independent foreign policy that asserted French sovereignty against both American and Soviet influence. He withdrew France from NATO's integrated military command in 1966 and pursued an independent nuclear deterrent, the force de frappe. In Japan, the Liberal Democratic Party, which governed almost without interruption from 1955 onward, combined conservative social values with state-directed economic development, guided by the Ministry of International Trade and Industry.

The end of the Cold War and the acceleration of globalization in the 1990s created the conditions for a revival of national conservatism as a self-conscious political movement. The expansion of the European Union, the growth of international trade agreements, and large-scale immigration to Western countries provoked a backlash among voters who felt that their national identities and economic prospects were being eroded by forces beyond their control. In Hungary, Viktor Orban transformed the Fidesz party from a liberal anti-communist movement into a national conservative governing force after his return to power in 2010. His government enacted a new constitution emphasizing Christian heritage, tightened immigration controls, and challenged the authority of European Union institutions.

The formalization of national conservatism as a coherent intellectual movement gained momentum with the publication of Yoram Hazony's The Virtue of Nationalism in 2018 and the convening of the first National Conservatism Conference in Washington, D.C. in July 2019. Subsequent conferences in Rome (2020), Brussels (2022), and London (2023) brought together politicians, academics, and writers from across the Western world. In the United States, figures such as Senator Josh Hawley and Governor Ron DeSantis have adopted positions associated with national conservatism, including skepticism of free trade agreements, opposition to large-scale immigration, and criticism of multinational corporations perceived as hostile to conservative cultural values.

Core Beliefs and Principles

The foundational commitment of national conservatism is to the nation-state as the highest and most legitimate form of political organization. National conservatives reject the transfer of sovereign authority to supranational institutions such as the European Union, the United Nations, or international trade bodies. They argue that democratic self-government is only meaningful within the borders of a distinct national community whose members share enough common ground in language, culture, and history to sustain genuine solidarity and mutual obligation. The nation-state, in this view, is not an arbitrary political construct but the natural outgrowth of shared identity formed over centuries.

Cultural continuity is a closely related principle. National conservatives hold that every nation possesses a distinctive cultural inheritance, shaped by its religious traditions, literary canon, legal customs, and social mores, and that the preservation of this inheritance is a primary duty of government. This conviction generates skepticism toward multiculturalism as a governing philosophy and opposition to immigration policies perceived as undermining the demographic and cultural character of the host nation. National conservatives distinguish between controlled immigration that serves the national interest and mass immigration that, in their analysis, weakens social cohesion.

Traditional social institutions, particularly the family, organized religion, and local community associations, occupy a central place in national conservative thought. These institutions are regarded as the building blocks of social order, transmitting values, fostering mutual responsibility, and providing the intermediate structures that stand between the individual and the state. Government policy, from taxation to education, should support and strengthen these institutions rather than displace them with bureaucratic alternatives.

National conservatives embrace economic nationalism as a corrective to what they view as the excesses of globalization. Free trade is acceptable where it benefits the nation, but it must be subordinated to broader considerations of national security, industrial self-sufficiency, and the welfare of domestic workers. Strategic tariffs, restrictions on foreign acquisition of critical industries, and active industrial policy are all legitimate tools of statecraft. This position places national conservatism in tension with the free-market orthodoxy that dominated Anglo-American conservatism from the 1980s through the early 2010s.

Economic Policy

National conservative economic policy occupies a middle ground between the state-directed command economies of the left and the laissez-faire capitalism advocated by classical liberals and libertarians. It accepts the market economy as the most efficient general mechanism for producing and distributing goods, but it insists that markets must serve the nation rather than the other way around. Where free trade undermines domestic industry, displaces workers, or creates dangerous dependencies on foreign suppliers, the state has a duty to intervene through tariffs, subsidies, and regulatory action.

Protective tariffs are the signature instrument of national conservative economic thought. Hamilton's original case for tariffs rested on the infant industry argument: that new domestic manufacturers needed temporary protection from established foreign competitors in order to develop the scale and expertise necessary to compete on equal terms. Modern national conservatives extend this reasoning to cover strategic industries such as semiconductor fabrication, pharmaceutical manufacturing, and energy production, where dependence on foreign supply chains poses risks to national security.

Industrial policy receives strong support within national conservative circles. Rather than leaving the allocation of capital entirely to market forces, national conservatives advocate for government direction of investment toward sectors deemed essential to national strength. This may include direct subsidies, tax incentives for domestic manufacturing, public investment in infrastructure, and procurement policies that favor domestic producers. National conservatives frequently cite the economic development models of postwar Japan, South Korea, and Singapore as evidence that strategic state intervention can accelerate industrialization without sacrificing market dynamism.

Labor market policy in the national conservative framework emphasizes the interests of domestic workers. Restrictions on immigration are justified not only on cultural grounds but also as a means of preventing wage suppression in sectors that employ lower-skilled labor. National conservatives tend to support strong enforcement of labor standards and, in some cases, minimum wage increases, distinguishing themselves from the supply-side economics that characterized mainstream conservative parties in the late 20th century.

Economic Position Summary

Market capitalism combined with strategic tariffs, economic nationalism, and state support for domestic industries.

Governance and Freedom

National conservative governance is distinguished by its emphasis on strong executive authority and centralized decision-making. National conservatives argue that effective government requires a clear locus of authority capable of acting decisively in defense of the national interest, whether in matters of foreign policy, border security, or domestic order. They tend to favor presidential or semi-presidential systems with strong executive powers over parliamentary arrangements that distribute authority more broadly. De Gaulle's construction of the Fifth Republic, with its powerful presidency and reduced role for the National Assembly, is a frequently cited model.

Border control and immigration enforcement are treated as fundamental obligations of the state. National conservatives view the ability to determine who enters the national territory as an essential attribute of sovereignty, without which self-government becomes meaningless. Policies typically associated with national conservative governance include physical barriers at border crossings, increased funding for border enforcement agencies, expedited deportation procedures for unauthorized residents, and reductions in legal immigration levels.

Law and order occupy a prominent place in the national conservative governing philosophy. National conservatives support well-funded police forces, firm sentencing guidelines, and policies that prioritize public safety over the rehabilitation of offenders. They are generally skeptical of criminal justice reform movements that seek to reduce incarceration rates, viewing such efforts as symptomatic of a broader cultural permissiveness that erodes social discipline.

Government policy toward education, media, and cultural institutions reflects the national conservative commitment to cultural continuity. National conservative governments have promoted curricula that emphasize national history, civic values, and the literary and religious traditions of the majority culture. They have also challenged the influence of what they describe as progressive cultural elites in universities, media organizations, and international NGOs, arguing that these institutions promote cosmopolitan values at the expense of national identity and social cohesion.

Governance Position Summary

Centralized state authority, strict border control, preservation of traditional family units, and law-and-order policing.

Notable Figures in National Conservatism

Alexander Hamilton

1755 - 1804

First U.S. Secretary of the Treasury

Hamilton championed a strong federal government, national banking system, and protective tariffs to develop American industry. His economic nationalism laid the groundwork for national conservative economic thought, favoring state-directed industrial policy over laissez-faire trade.

Charles de Gaulle

1890 - 1970

President of France (1959-1969)

De Gaulle embodied national conservatism through his insistence on French sovereignty, his creation of the Fifth Republic with its strong presidency, and his independent foreign policy that included withdrawing France from NATO's military command. He combined conservative social values with state-directed economic modernization.

Viktor Orban

1963 - present

Prime Minister of Hungary

Orban has become one of the most prominent contemporary national conservatives, advocating for what he calls 'illiberal democracy.' His government has pursued strict immigration controls, promotion of traditional family values, and resistance to European Union centralization while maintaining Hungary's EU membership.

Yoram Hazony

1964 - present

Israeli philosopher and political theorist

Hazony authored The Virtue of Nationalism (2018), which provided a modern intellectual framework for national conservatism. He organized the National Conservatism Conference in 2019, creating an institutional platform for the movement and arguing that the nation-state is the best framework for human flourishing.
