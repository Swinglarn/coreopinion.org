Communism

What is communism? Explore the history of Marxist-Leninist states, the core beliefs of classless society and central planning, and its position on the political compass.

Overview

Communism is a revolutionary political and economic ideology that aims to establish a classless, stateless, and moneyless society based on the common ownership of the means of production. Rooted in the works of Karl Marx and Friedrich Engels, communism posits that capitalism inherently exploits the working class (proletariat) and must be replaced by a socialist transitional phase, which eventually matures into a fully realized communist society.

In political theory, communism represents the ultimate collectivist and egalitarian system. It advocates for the abolition of private property, which is replaced by public ownership of all factories, land, and natural resources. The economy is organized not by market forces of supply and demand, but by centralized state planning designed to meet the collective needs of the population rather than individual profit.

On the political compass, communism is situated at the extreme far-left edge of the economic axis, reflecting its complete rejection of capitalism and private enterprise. Historically, communist states (modeled on Marxist-Leninist principles) have combined this far-left economics with highly centralized, authoritarian governance, placing them in the upper-left quadrant of the compass.

Historical Development

The modern communist movement began with the publication of The Communist Manifesto by Marx and Engels in 1848, which called for the international unification of the working class. It remained a primarily intellectual and minor political movement until the Russian Revolution of 1917, when the Bolsheviks, led by Vladimir Lenin, overthrew the Russian Provisional Government and established the Russian Soviet Federative Socialist Republic, which later formed the Soviet Union in 1922.

Following Lenin's death in 1924, Joseph Stalin consolidated power and implemented a policy of "socialism in one country." He launched the first Five-Year Plan in 1928, initiating rapid, forced industrialization and the collectivization of agriculture. This process generated significant industrial expansion, enabling the USSR to defeat Nazi Germany in World War II, but it resulted in millions of deaths from political purges and famines, such as the Holodomor in Ukraine.

After World War II, communism expanded into Eastern Europe, forming the Warsaw Pact bloc, and spread to Asia. The Chinese Communist Party, led by Mao Zedong, won the Chinese Civil War in 1949, establishing the People's Republic of China. The Cold War between the United States and the Soviet Union dominated global politics for four decades, characterized by proxy wars in Korea, Vietnam, and Angola, and the Cuban Missile Crisis of 1962.

Economic stagnation, consumer shortages, and political repression gradually eroded the legitimacy of communist regimes. In 1989, popular protests led to the fall of the Berlin Wall and the collapse of communist governments throughout Eastern Europe. The Soviet Union was formally dissolved in December 1991, leaving China, Cuba, Vietnam, Laos, and North Korea as the remaining countries ruled by communist parties, though most have introduced market reforms to stimulate growth.

Core Beliefs and Principles

The foundation of communist theory is historical materialism, developed by Marx. This philosophy asserts that the economic structure of a society—the way goods are produced and exchanged—determines its political, legal, and cultural institutions. History is viewed as a series of class struggles between the owners of the means of production (the bourgeoisie in capitalist society) and the workers (the proletariat) who must sell their labor for wages.

Marx argued that capitalism contains inherent contradictions that lead to its collapse. Industrialization concentrates workers in large factories, fostering class consciousness, while the pursuit of profit leads to economic crises of overproduction and the impoverishment of the working class. This process makes a proletarian revolution inevitable.

The "dictatorship of the proletariat" is the transitional phase immediately following the revolution. During this stage, the working class uses state power to suppress capitalist resistance and nationalize private property. Once class distinctions are abolished, the state is theorized to gradually "wither away," as the administration of people is replaced by the administration of things, resulting in a stateless society guided by the principle: "From each according to his ability, to each according to his need."

Democratic centralism is the organizational principle of communist parties, formulated by Lenin. It combines freedom of discussion during the policy-formulation phase with absolute unity of action once a decision has been made. In practice, this principle concentrated decision-making power in the hands of the party leadership (the Politburo) and suppressed internal dissent, creating a highly disciplined and top-down political structure.

"The abolition of private property and class divisions through collective ownership of the means of production is necessary to establish a classless, stateless society."

Economic Policy

Communist economic policy is characterized by the complete rejection of market capitalism and the establishment of a state-managed command economy. All means of production, including land, factories, mines, and banks, are nationalized and operated as state property or collective cooperatives.

Central planning replaced the market price mechanism. The state, through central planning agencies like Gosplan in the Soviet Union, set production targets, allocated raw materials, and determined prices for all goods and services. These plans were typically organized into five-year cycles, prioritizing heavy industry, infrastructure, and military production over consumer goods, leading to chronic shortages of food, clothing, and housing.

Agriculture was systematically collectivized. In the late 1920s and 1930s, individual peasant farms were merged into large state-run enterprises (sovkhozy) or collective farms (kolkhozy). This consolidation aimed to increase efficiency through mechanization and secure grain supplies for urban industrial workers, but it faced resistance from peasants, leading to the slaughter of livestock, agricultural collapse, and widespread famines.

Private employment was banned, making the state the sole employer. Wages were set by state administrators based on job category and productivity, and social welfare programs, including universal healthcare, free education, subsidized housing, and guaranteed retirement pensions, were funded directly by the state, aiming to guarantee a minimum standard of living for all citizens.

Economic Position Summary

Complete state or public ownership of all resources and enterprises, total elimination of market mechanism and wage labor.

Governance and Freedom

Governance in communist states is based on a one-party system, where the communist party holds a constitutional monopoly on political power. The party operates parallel to the state administration, with party members holding all key positions in the government, military, judiciary, and civil service, ensuring absolute policy alignment.

The legislative branch, typically a national assembly like the Supreme Soviet, met briefly to ratify decisions already made by the party leadership. Elections were non-competitive, with voters presented with a single list of candidates approved by the party. The executive branch was controlled by the Premier and the General Secretary of the Party, who often concentrated supreme power in their own hands.

State security was maintained by a specialized secret police force, such as the Cheka, NKVD, or KGB. These agencies monitored the population, suppressed political opposition, and enforced ideological conformity. Under Stalin, this security apparatus conducted the Great Purge (1936-1938), executing hundreds of thousands of party members, military officers, and citizens suspected of disloyalty, and sending millions to the Gulag network of forced labor camps.

Public discourse, education, and media were subjected to total state control. Censorship was active, and all newspapers, publishing houses, and broadcasting stations were operated directly by the state or party organizations. Education was utilized to promote Marxist-Leninist ideology, and membership in state-run youth organizations, like the Komsomol, was encouraged to foster ideological loyalty among the younger generation.

Governance Position Summary

Single-party dictatorship of the proletariat, centralized state control, and the planned transition toward a stateless, classless society.

Notable Figures in Communism

Karl Marx

1818 - 1883

Philosopher and co-author of The Communist Manifesto

Marx formulated the materialist conception of history, arguing that human societies progress through class struggle. His critique of capitalism in Das Kapital laid the economic foundation for communism, asserting that the working class would inevitably overthrow the capitalist class and establish a classless society based on common ownership.

Friedrich Engels

1820 - 1895

Social scientist and co-author of The Communist Manifesto

Engels partnered with Marx to develop scientific socialism, financially supporting Marx's research and co-writing major theoretical works. After Marx's death, he edited and published the second and third volumes of Das Kapital, and authored Socialism: Utopian and Scientific, making Marxism accessible to a global audience.

Vladimir Lenin

1870 - 1924

Leader of the Bolshevik Revolution and founder of the Soviet Union

Lenin adapted Marxist theory to the conditions of early 20th century Russia, arguing that a disciplined vanguard party was necessary to lead the working class to revolution. He led the Bolshevik seizure of power in October 1917 and established the world's first socialist state, implementing war communism and later the New Economic Policy before his death in 1924.

Joseph Stalin

1878 - 1953

General Secretary of the Communist Party of the Soviet Union

Stalin consolidated power after Lenin's death, launching rapid industrialization and agricultural collectivization in the late 1920s. His regime defeated Nazi Germany in World War II but was marked by totalitarian control, political purges, the Gulag forced labor system, and widespread famine resulting from economic reorganization.
